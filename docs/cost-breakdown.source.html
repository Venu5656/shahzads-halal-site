The software is the cheap part

At 3,000 orders a month the whole technology bill is about $2,726 — and $2,640 of that is the card fee, which only exists because money came in. The software that runs the site, the database, the receipts and the alerts costs $86 a month, about 3% of the bill. Doubling your orders roughly doubles the card fee and barely moves anything else.

Put another way: on a $20 order you pay 88¢ to take the money — 58¢ on the percentage, 30¢ flat. That is 4.4% of the order. On a $40 order it is $1.46, or 3.7%: the flat 30¢ hurts small orders most, so anything that raises the average basket pays for itself twice.

Worth doing: ask whoever processes your in-store cards what they charge. You are already paying a rate like this on counter sales, and comparing the two tells you whether online ordering is adding a cost or just moving where it lands.

One-off costs you pay directly

  • Domain name — about $15 a year, bought in your name so you own it.
  • Stripe account — free to open. No setup fee, no monthly fee, no closure fee. Needs the business name, EIN and bank details.
  • Kitchen printer — only if the shop does not already have one that can be reached over the network. Cost depends on which option you pick; we will price it once you have decided.
  • Disputes — $15 each if a customer charges back, win or lose. Rare on pickup orders, but not zero.

What this price does not include

  • A lawyer reading the terms and privacy pages. Drafts are written and live, and they honestly describe what the software does with customer data — but they are marked as drafts and are not binding until someone qualified signs them off.
  • Photography for 12 dishes. They currently show a branded tile rather than a photo. It looks deliberate rather than broken, but a photo sells food.
  • Ongoing support after launch, as above.

What is holding up the rest

The remaining $1,850 is not waiting on time — it is waiting on four things only you can provide:

  • A Stripe account with the developer added as a team member. The test keys alone unblock all the payment work; the business entity, EIN and bank account are only needed before the first real charge.
  • A domain name, and control of its DNS. Apple Pay will not work without one, and receipts sent from a borrowed domain land in spam.
  • A printer decision, and whether there is a spare PC or tablet in the shop.
  • Two price questions. The board prices the Chicken & Lamb gyro at $9.99 while you said $13.99, and the board charges $2 to leave the salad off a platter while you said it should be free. The site currently follows the board on the first and you on the second. Both are a phone call to settle.
